The [system design](/explanation/architecture/) explains what the pieces are. This
page explains the ideas behind them, so that when you build a workflow you are
working with the grain of the system, not against it.

## Success is earned, not claimed

An agent does not get to declare itself done. A step completes only when it meets
its success rule: first the hard checks (did it actually fetch the required data,
and does that data carry the fields you asked for?), then a judgment on whether it
answered the task.

This matters because a language model will happily produce a confident, tidy
paragraph with nothing behind it. The rule makes that impossible. A price-check
step cannot pass without real price data that actually includes a last price. That
is what turns a chat model into an accountable step you can trust.

<Aside type="tip">
This is why the guides push you toward evidence-backed rules on every agent. A minimum word count is not a rule worth having; required evidence is.
</Aside>

## Collect once, reason many times

Evidence gathered by one agent flows downstream to the next as real data. A later
analyst can be held to a rule about data an earlier collector fetched, without
touching the tool itself.

So you do not fetch the same price in every agent. One collector fetches it, and
every agent after it reasons over the same evidence. This is the shape of the
[Two-Step Pipeline](/guides/patterns/#two-step-pipeline) and the
[Parallel Research Tracks](/guides/patterns/#parallel-research-tracks).

## A clear role for every agent

Each agent has a short, clear role, like Data Collector, Risk Critic, or Briefing
Synthesizer. The role is the agent's anchor: it is the one thing that always stays
in view, even when the workflow trims bulky upstream data to fit. The step-by-step
instructions are the procedure; the role is the job. Keeping them apart is what
lets the agent stay on task.

This is why the [authoring guide](/guides/author-a-schema/) asks for tight role
names and procedural instructions.

## Your settings always win

Every value that can be set, like the provider, the model, and the key, comes from
one place: your Settings or your workflow. There is no second, hidden default in a
lower layer quietly overriding what you chose.

- **No hidden model.** If a run needs a key you have not set, it says so plainly. It
  does not fall back to a shared key and bill someone else. The honest failure is
  the point.
- **Nothing guessed behind your back.** What you set is what runs.

## Fail honestly

When a step cannot finish, the system does not pretend it did. Retries are limited,
a step that runs out of tries becomes a clean failure instead of crashing the run,
and you choose whether the rest of the workflow keeps going or stops at the first
failure. Either way, the run's status tells the truth about what happened.

## Explicit and legible

There is no hidden control flow. You place the agents, you draw the connections,
and loops are kept simple enough that the order of work is always well defined. The
canvas shows exactly the workflow that runs.

The payoff is legibility. Anyone can look at the canvas and know the order of the
steps and what depends on what, without running it. That legibility is the whole
point of a canvas over hand-written agent code.
